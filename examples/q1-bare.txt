The 30 km/h citywide speed limit has gained traction across European cities — Brussels, Paris, Bilbao, Helsinki — and the safety case is strong. World Health Organization data show that the probability of pedestrian fatality in a collision drops from roughly 50% at 50 km/h to about 10% at 30 km/h, driven by the non-linear relationship between vehicle speed and impact energy.

Implementation, however, requires more than changing signs. Cities that have succeeded combined the limit with physical interventions — narrower lanes, raised crossings, traffic calming — because drivers tend to drive at the speed the road environment encourages, not what signs prescribe. Studies show enforcement-by-camera works better than discretionary policing.

The economic argument is mixed. Business associations often cite reduced delivery efficiency; municipal governments cite lower healthcare costs from fewer injuries and improved livability. Surveys after implementation tend to show rising approval over time as residents experience the change.

Overall, the evidence supports adoption with complementary infrastructure, though local context matters — a dense historic center differs from a sprawling suburb.
