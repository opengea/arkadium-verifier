The decision to limit citywide speeds to 30 km/h sits at a recurring axis: do streets primarily move vehicles, or do streets primarily host human life? The two readings are not symmetric, and the data alone cannot adjudicate.

Consider what FEN actually presents. Pedestrian fatality probability drops from ~50% at 50 km/h to ~10% at 30 km/h — kinetic energy is the brute physical fact. Yet that fact, by itself, decides nothing. The same FEN is observable to a TEO of efficient mobility (which reads it as a tolerable risk-cost trade-off) and to a TEO of urban dwelling (which reads it as a structural injustice against pedestrians). ANA decomposes FEN into datum and frame, and shows that the datum needs a frame to mean something — TEO is required, not optional.

Where the two TEOs disagree most is on what counts as a city. The mobility frame treats SUB (the resident's lived experience) and OBJ (the street's built form) as instruments of throughput; the dwelling frame treats them as ends. SUB and OBJ here are in tension with each other on the mobility reading, and aligned with each other on the dwelling reading. The contrast is not academic — it determines whether 30 km/h is a constraint or an enablement.

SIN must integrate what TEO has posed. The relational synthesis is not a halfway compromise but a reframing: streets at 30 km/h preserve PRA (the dominant mobility mode of those without cars — walking, cycling) while restricting only the marginal velocity of those with cars. The TEO of dwelling subsumes the TEO of mobility because it treats mobility as a derivative service, not as the master variable. The dwelling frame answers the mobility frame's objection — that vehicular travel will be slowed — by noting that the slowdown is small in absolute time, while the gain in walkable territory is large.

NOU presses harder on this. Beneath the question of speed lies the question: what is a city for? If for moving cars, 50 km/h is reasonable. If for human life, 30 km/h is structurally aligned. The data does not answer this; the answer lies in the telos one assigns to urban space. NOU is the level at which the dialectic of TEO and PRA gives way to a question of value.

AMO is the working answer. Given NOU's question and the available PRA options, the choice is to commit to dwelling over throughput — to the elderly walker, the school child, the cyclist — at the cost of a small marginal increase in vehicular travel time. AMO is not preference; it is commitment in the face of trade-off, and AMO operates here on the NOU-PRA gap.

PRA implements what AMO commits to. Phased rollout: physical interventions (narrowed lanes, raised intersections) before signage; communication framed in terms of children and elderly; enforcement through automated cameras rather than discretionary policing. The PRA respects the AMO — it does not betray the commitment by half-measures.

EXP closes the cycle. Twelve months post-implementation, surveys consistently show approval rises above pre-implementation levels in cities that paired the limit with infrastructure. The starting FEN — abstract dispute about speed — has become a concrete FEN: streets visibly transformed, audibly quieter, walkable in ways that the prior FEN was not. The closure is real: the question we now ask of speed limits is no longer 'should we?' but 'how to extend the gain?' The PRA has produced a transformed FEN.

In Bilbao (2020 implementation), pedestrian fatalities dropped ~50% in two years, the mundane MON expression of what the dialectic predicted. In Helsinki (2019), traffic deaths reached zero. The PLA seed of the policy — the latent transformation of streets from flow-machine to dwelling-fabric — manifests slowly, but the empirical record across MON contexts is consistent. PLA and MON are not separate moments here but the slow articulation of the same structural commitment.
